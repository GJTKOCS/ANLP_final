Agents that share context can talk privately in public: a third party who lacks that context cannot decode what they mean.
We study whether a small language model can \emph{learn} this skill. Agent A knows a secret (who will bring the birthday person, and where the party is)
and must convey it to B, who shares eight private memories with A, while chatting about an unrelated topic in front of C, who does not know the memories.
We train A with multi-turn GRPO against listeners that are retrained every round: the outsider C has the same architecture, data and training as B's listener
and lacks only the memories, which are freshly generated for every episode before the secret is drawn, so no reusable code exists.
Reward is high when B's listener is confident in the truth, C's is not, and the chat is coherent and names nothing.

\section{Introduction}
People routinely say things in public that only their friends understand. A reference to ``the ER saga'' means nothing to a stranger and everything to
the person who was in the waiting room with you. Clark and Schaefer showed that speakers shape what they say so that overhearers cannot follow it
while addressees can~\cite{clark1987concealing}, and Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs framed reference itself as a collaborative process grounded in shared history~\cite{clark1986referring}.
Language-model agents increasingly share context with each other (memory, tools, earlier turns), so the same phenomenon is a safety concern: a monitor that
reads only the visible messages may fail to understand a coordinated exchange~\cite{motwani2024secret,mathew2024hidden}.

We ask a concrete question: \emph{can a small model be trained to speak privately in public, using shared history as the key, against an outsider that keeps learning?}
We build a game (Section~\ref{sec:task}) in which A must convey a two-part secret to B in a short, natural conversation about an unrelated cover topic, in the presence of C.
The shared history is a bank of eight memories regenerated for every episode and written \emph{before} the secret is drawn from the people and places it mentions.
Any trick that does not depend on the memories (a reused phrase, a public clue such as ``chai'' suggesting a cafe) is learnable by C,
because C is trained on exactly the same transcripts as B's listener. Training therefore pushes A toward the one strategy C cannot copy.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) A task and data generator for covert dialogue over fresh shared history with strict information boundaries enforced in one module.
(ii) An adversarial training method: multi-turn GRPO for the speaker, with listeners (insider and outsider) retrained every round on cumulative transcripts,
following Kerckhoffs's principle~\cite{kerckhoffs1883}. (iii) An evaluation protocol in which only the speaker changes between conditions, and every condition gets a fresh, adapted insider and outsider.
